Caravaggio

We never called ourselves "Baroque." That name was slapped on us later by art historians. There are all kinds of theories—it might come from the Portuguese word for an "irregular pearl," or something like that—but apparently it wasn't meant as a compliment. The nerve of those guys… Wanna get knocked flat?!

Point①
An artistic era as long-lasting and influential as the Renaissance.

Velazquez

Baroque was one of the longest-lasting artistic periods in the history of art. Because it developed across Europe over such a long span of time, it isn't a style you can simply sum up by saying, "This is what Baroque art looks like!" Take me, for example, who worked in Spain, and Rubens, who flourished in Flanders—we couldn't be more different in style. That's the real charm of Baroque: each region developed its own unique artistic character.

Point②
One shared hallmark is its dramatic use of light and dynamic poses.

Rembrandt

Velázquez may put it that way, but there are some things most Baroque artists have in common. The biggest one is the dramatic use of light and bold poses! We wanted to create scenes that felt even more dramatic than reality itself and stir people's emotions. And it was during the Baroque period that artists really began depicting the unique qualities of light at different times of day.

Point③
Following the Renaissance, artistic styles became even more diverse.

Rubens

The scientific and philosophical ideas that flourished during the Renaissance branched out even further in the Baroque era! This was when genres like vanitas, which reflects on the fleeting nature of life, and trompe-l'œil, paintings that fool the eye into believing they're real, first came into their own. Art may come in many forms, but when it comes to technical mastery, Baroque is still number one! Ohohohoho!

This is the Baroque piece!

Supper at Emmaus

This painting depicts the moment after the Crucifixion when two of Jesus Christ's disciples, dining in the town of Emmaus with a young traveler they had met on the road, suddenly realize that the young man is the resurrected Christ himself. The contrast between the disciples, who lunge forward in astonishment, and Christ, who remains quietly seated, is heightened by striking chiaroscuro and a powerful composition, making it one of the defining masterpieces of the Baroque style.
